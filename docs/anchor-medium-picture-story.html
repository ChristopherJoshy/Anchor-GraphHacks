Two Backup Suppliers, One Hidden Failure

How we built a supply-chain recovery agent that follows hidden dependencies, finds a workable plan, and checks whether it actually happened.

The cheapest recovery proposal in our demo cost $2,220. It appeared to cover all 1,000 microcontrollers. Then we followed the supplier dependencies: both backup suppliers relied on the factory that had just failed.

That is the problem behind Anchor, built by Team Nexus for Graph Hacks 2026, Track 01 — Agents That Act on Connected Data. Finding another supplier is only the beginning. We also need to ask whether it shares the disruption, whether its capacity meets the order's constraints, what could break the proposed recovery next, and whether the simulated action actually changed operational state.

The story follows one order from the first sign of trouble to the final check. We start with the web interface, look under the hood at FalkorDB, follow the calculation in Codex, and then check the stored result. Along the way, we explain what each screen helped us understand and where the prototype still falls short.

Everything operational in the demo is synthetic. Suppliers, factories, contracts, capacities, orders, prices, routes, and incidents are fictional. Geographic reference points provide context. No real procurement, payment, logistics, or production system receives writes.

The Silicon Cascade: why two backups can be one failure

A fictional manufacturer needs 1,000 microcontrollers. Its warehouse holds 350 usable units, leaving 650 to source. An upstream foundry in Taiwan goes offline. Two cheaper suppliers, B and C, look attractive when viewed as isolated records.

A dependency-blind cheapest-first proposal chooses 350 inventory units, 600 from B, and 50 from C. The nominal incremental cost is $2,220. That arithmetic is internally consistent, but its availability assumption is wrong.

FalkorDB returns dependency paths from both B and C to the failed foundry. Separate supplier identities do not establish independent supply. Once that evidence is applied, neither allocation contributes eligible capacity under this incident. Only the 350 inventory units remain, leaving 650 unmet.

This is the graph's decisive contribution. A relationship changes which capacity can enter the feasible solution. The graph picture helps explain the result, but the exclusion has to reach the optimizer too.

Following the connection that changes the answer

Here is the moment the graph becomes useful. In this recorded Codex session, Anchor calls the incident, assessment, and planning tools. The returned paths show that the primary source and both cheap backups lead to the same upstream foundry. Read the path lines first, then compare them with the allocation and exclusion explanation below.

FalkorDB is Anchor's primary dependency graph and evidence/history store. The model represents suppliers, facilities, products, inventory, orders, contracts, locations, routes, incidents, plans, actions, and receipts. Relationships express dependencies, supply, demand, approval, and decision provenance.

When an incident is created, Anchor changes the modeled availability and capacity of the affected entity and relevant supply or production edge. Bounded traversals find affected demand and shared upstream dependencies. The planner retrieves candidate sources and their evidence, applies qualification rules, and assembles graph-linked failure scenarios.

The explanation preserves why a source was excluded, including its path to the incident entity. Plans retain their graph version. Actions and receipts remain connected to the incident and plan, making the history retrievable after reopening the coding-agent client.

There is no parallel in-memory supply graph to quietly take over. If FalkorDB is unavailable, graph-dependent work fails clearly. A separate integration test removes a predefined dependency edge and checks that the chosen recovery changes. This is our practical test of whether connected data is doing useful work.

Agent-facing tools accept typed, bounded domain inputs. They do not expose arbitrary Cypher, SQL, shell commands, or unrestricted file access. The backend uses fixed parameterized graph queries.

The database behind the pictures: how the records connect

The globe is the easy part to see. The database has to explain why two suppliers that look separate can fail together. In the diagram below, each box is a type of record and each named arrow tells us how two records are connected. For example, Supplier → DEPENDS_ON → Supplier means one supplier relies on another. The drawing uses the relationship names in the code; it is a schema explanation, not a live database screenshot.

Dependency and qualification: Contract → APPROVES → Supplier records qualification. Supplier → DEPENDS_ON → Supplier represents upstream exposure. In this fixture, the Taiwan foundry's operational source is a Supplier node; its physical facility is a separate Facility node reached through OPERATES. That distinction explains why the returned B/C failure paths use supplier IDs even though the story describes a factory outage.

Supply and demand: Supplier → SUPPLIES → Product carries capacity, unit price, currency, lead days, and availability. Order → REQUIRES → Product identifies demand. Inventory → STORES → Product and Inventory → HELD_AT → Facility connect usable stock to the product and its modeled storage context. Contract and supply records help qualify capacity before the solver assigns quantities.

Geography and wider production: Supplier → OPERATES → Facility → LOCATED_AT → Location gives the inspector its geographic context. The full seed also includes Facility → PRODUCES → Product, Facility → REQUIRES_INPUT → Product, and RouteLeg → STARTS_AT / ENDS_AT → Location. Those relationships populate the wider connected model; their existence does not mean the current solver implements a complete multi-facility production or transport schedule.

The seed labels are Supplier, Facility, Product, Inventory, Order, Customer, Location, Contract, RouteLeg, Incident, Evidence, RecoveryPlan, Action, ExecutionReceipt, Scenario, Policy, and Organization. Stable id properties identify records. Provenance and timestamps distinguish the scenario and evidence context. Scenario.version advances on material changes; a plan captures the version used to compute it.

Keeping the decision, action, and receipt together

The network explains why a source was chosen. A second set of connections explains what Anchor tried to do about it. Follow the plan to its actions, then follow each action to its receipt.

RecoveryPlan → MITIGATES → Incident links the recommendation to its disruption. RecoveryPlan → CONTAINS → Action and Action → PRODUCED → ExecutionReceipt connect the planned intervention to recorded attempts. Incident → SUPPORTED_BY → Evidence and Action → JUSTIFIED_BY → Evidence are supported schema relationships; the implementation persists plan/action/receipt links for the demonstrated workflow. The schema's available relationship types alone do not establish that every new runtime action receives every possible evidence edge.

This structure helps a later client retrieve the decision trail by incident, plan, action, and run IDs. It also keeps the verification boundary clear: FalkorDB stores linked evidence and history, while SQLite stores the mock ERP's mutable allocations. A graph receipt is not a substitute for reading the ERP state back.

The dependency query follows bounded DEPENDS_ON paths toward the affected source and returns node IDs along each path. Candidate qualification uses those paths to exclude compromised suppliers. The exact fixed parameterized Cypher templates are published in the repository's query reference; the client cannot supply arbitrary queries.

Using Codex to work through the same recovery

The web view helps us see the situation. Codex lets us ask for the next step in ordinary language. Before making a change, the recorded session resets the synthetic example and checks its network state. That baseline check matters: otherwise a previous reservation could make a later attempt look like a new recovery.

Codex CLI supplies natural-language interpretation and MCP tool selection. Anchor's Python backend owns validation, graph access, constraint enforcement, orchestration, and verification. OR-Tools CP-SAT computes integer allocations. A persistent SQLite mock ERP owns the simulated operational mutations.

Anchor's backend does not call an LLM API to invent quantities or operational facts. The host coding agent can ask which order is exposed and request a recovery, but the answer must come from graph state and deterministic computation.

The MCP tool set includes health, network inspection, incident creation, cascade assessment, planning, simulated execution, verification, explanation, and persisted history. Simulation modification tools allow controlled capacity and dependency changes. Unsupported inputs are rejected. Codex CLI was exercised; compatibility with other clients should not be assumed merely because they support MCP.

How Anchor works out the quantities

In plain language, the solver decides how many units each eligible source should send to the order. It works in whole units. Each allocation variable represents that quantity from one source to one order. For each order, allocated quantity plus explicit unmet quantity equals demand. For each source, allocations cannot exceed capacity. Inventory is a zero-price source in the flagship fixture.

Availability, contract approval, product compatibility, and the lead-time deadline are hard eligibility rules. The optimizer cannot buy its way around an unapproved supplier by accepting a penalty. The current cost model requires one currency and minor-unit scale across the candidate sources; the fixture uses USD cents.

We gave those decisions an order of importance. Anchor first tries to cover the orders, then tries to protect that coverage in the tested failure cases, and only then looks for a cheaper, simpler allocation. In the code, this means solving these five objectives in sequence and keeping each best result fixed before moving on:

  1. Minimize current priority-weighted unmet demand.
  2. Minimize the worst priority-weighted shortfall across the selected single-failure scenarios.
  3. Minimize the worst priority-weighted shortfall across the selected correlated-pair scenarios.
  4. Minimize incremental allocation cost.
  5. Minimize newly activated supplier count.

Priority-weighted unmet demand means an order's priority multiplied by its unfilled units, summed across orders. It is a model score, not a failure probability. Sequential objectives preserve the ordering: reducing cost cannot worsen a previously fixed service optimum.

The implementation uses one solver worker, a fixed random seed, and a 20-second limit per lexicographic solve. It requires CP-SAT to return OPTIMAL; otherwise it raises rather than presenting an unproven optimum as established. This solver status concerns the declared mathematical model, not the completeness or truth of real-world supply data.

The recovery numbers, including the expensive part

No supplier intervention: 350 inventory units, 650 unmet, $0 incremental sourcing cost. That is a shortage, not recovery.

Dependency-blind cheapest proposal: 350 inventory + 600 B + 50 C, nominal cost $2,220. Graph qualification leaves 650 units unmet. This is a deliberately defined baseline that ignores dependencies, not a comparison against every spreadsheet or procurement product.

Graph-aware recovery: 350 inventory + 650 independent D, zero current unmet units, $5,850 synthetic incremental cost.

D reduced to 450: 350 inventory + 450 D + 200 independent E, zero current unmet units, $6,550 synthetic incremental cost.

Then we changed the question: what if D can supply only 450 units? The next frame shows the same workflow calculating again. Look for E entering the allocation, then read the final order state and the note about the existing inventory reservation.

The revised plan costs $700 more than the first feasible plan. The point is not a claimed saving: the cheaper proposal was unavailable under the disruption. The same solver recomputes from the changed capacity; no supplier-name conditional tells it to choose E.

We also evaluate an intentionally infeasible setting: D, E, and F have zero usable capacity. The model reports 650 units unmet. A useful recovery system needs to expose that result rather than manufacture a success.

Testing what might fail after the recovery

A plan that works under the current outage can still be vulnerable to another connected failure. Anchor assembles an explicit finite candidate set: single-source outages and selected supplier pairs with a shared upstream dependency. Graph-supplied correlated scenarios carry paths so a reviewer can inspect why the failures are connected.

The loop starts with the current recovery allocation. For each scenario it has not yet added to the plan, it asks how much of the lost supply could be replaced. The technical name for that replacement step is recourse. Surviving first-stage allocations still consume their source capacity; replacement quantities may use only surviving unused capacity that meets the same eligibility rules.

If a scenario leaves priority-weighted unmet demand, the worst unseen case is added to the optimization model and Anchor solves again. The default limit is eight added scenarios; the MCP interface accepts a bounded iteration input from zero to sixteen. The loop stops when no unseen cases remain, no unseen case has positive shortfall, or the configured addition limit is reached. The returned plan reports the enumerated cases, added iteration count, worst counterexample when present, residual demand, and assumptions. The current payload does not have a separate named termination-reason field.

The final evaluator scores the entire enumerated candidate set even when the addition limit is reached. A stress pass requires both zero current unmet demand and zero shortfall across all enumerated cases. Zero added iterations can therefore mean the initial allocation already passed the tested cases; it does not mean every possible outage was searched.

The flagship single-order benchmark passed its nine declared scenarios. It does not demonstrate that a counterexample changed that allocation. A separate focused two-order optimizer test does: one cheap source and one more expensive source can initially satisfy both orders, but a path-bearing failure of the expensive source changes which order receives the scarce cheap supply. The revised allocation protects the higher-priority order and leaves a weighted residual shortfall of one in the worst tested case. The test checks that one scenario was added and retains its evidence path.

That distinction matters. We have a passing single-order stress fixture and a separate test where a counterexample changes priority allocation. Neither establishes universal resilience. There are no estimated disruption probabilities, no formal proof against arbitrary failures, and no claim that missing dependencies can be inferred reliably.

A receipt is a starting point. The stored state is the check.

Now look at the receipt column in the next frame. Both rows say DUPLICATE. That tells us these reservations already existed. The read-back is still useful—it confirms the active quantities—but we should not tell the reader that this attempt created fresh supply.

Execution is confined to SIMULATION mode. Before applying a plan, Anchor checks its graph version and current mock ERP state. Idempotency handling prevents a repeated request from being counted as a fresh reservation. An existing successful reservation can produce a DUPLICATE receipt, which is useful confirmation but not evidence of new quantities applied.

The mock ERP persists reservations and allocations independently of the response text. Anchor reads that stored state back and compares postconditions with the intended plan. Verification mismatches remain failures; an optimistic receipt alone is insufficient.

The failure-injection path rejects one reservation for a chosen synthetic supplier. The integration test checks the unsuccessful attempt, false verification, and its 650-unit remaining shortfall, then one bounded replan and a verified second attempt. The caller can set max_replans to zero to stop after the failed action; the supported retry budget is at most one replan.

FalkorDB and SQLite are not one atomic distributed transaction. This prototype does not implement general partial-workflow compensation, timeout/backoff orchestration, or multi-process concurrency guarantees. Failures and partial outcomes have to remain visible in the history. The verification tool reads current ERP state; it is not a complete historical replay system.

How we checked whether Anchor was doing a useful job

We did not want to judge the project by whether the answer sounded convincing. We used the following practical checks. These are our own engineering criteria, rather than the event’s official weighted judging rubric. The official event rules require FalkorDB to serve a central function and require supporting implementation materials. Our evaluation translates Anchor's own product claims into checks a reviewer can inspect.

1. Graph indispensability. Does a dependency relationship affect eligibility and the selected recovery? Inspect the B/C exclusion paths and the integration test that removes a dependency edge. A plausible network visualization alone does not satisfy this criterion.

2. Allocation correctness. Do integer quantities obey source capacities, demand balance, approvals, compatibility, and deadlines? Focused optimizer tests cover both the 350 + 650 baseline and the 350 + 450 + 200 capacity change, as well as unavailable, unapproved, wrong-product, and late sources.

3. Honest infeasibility. Does insufficient eligible capacity produce explicit unmet demand? The deliberately infeasible benchmark leaves 650 units unmet. Solver optimality with a positive unmet quantity must not be described as a recovered order.

4. Bounded resilience and counterexample influence. Are the tested scenarios and limits explicit? Does a supplied graph-path counterexample change a priority allocation in a focused test? Is remaining shortfall still reported after the bound? A finite pass is not a universal safety claim.

5. Action integrity. Does execution change persisted state, detect a rejection, preserve failed attempts, and confirm postconditions by read-back? The ERP tests cover persistence, insufficient capacity, one-shot rejection, duplicates, conflicts, release behavior, and verification mismatch. The integration test exercises the graph-to-ERP workflow.

6. State and tool boundaries. Are simulation mode, supported input types, stale versions, and bounded replanning enforced in code? Can a later client session retrieve persisted decisions and evidence? The test suite and domain-tool schemas provide the evidence; broad production security or distributed correctness is outside this evaluation.

7. Reproducibility and performance transparency. Are dataset size, machine, sample counts, timing boundaries, and limitations available? We publish a local benchmark script and dated results. We do not treat a small warm laptop benchmark as a production service-level promise.

8. Demonstration honesty. Can the reader distinguish explanatory art, recorded tool output, synthetic scenario values, benchmark measurements, and unimplemented plans? We disclose duplicate receipts, separate runs, and the fact that the recorded demo does not show every tested failure path.

How to reproduce the prototype locally

Start with the Anchor repository and its README. The backend requires Python 3.12 or 3.13, uv, and Docker with Compose. The optional web explorer also requires Node.js 22.18 or newer and npm.

Follow the README’s setup commands in order: copy the example environment file, start FalkorDB with Docker Compose, install the Python dependencies, seed the demo, and start the backend. The reset command replaces the named synthetic graph and mock ERP, so preserve any local scenario history you want first.

The backend exposes MCP on loopback port 8765 at the /mcp path. Use the exact Codex registration command in the README, then run Anchor’s doctor command to check readiness. To use the browser companion, follow the web setup commands there and open the local address printed by the development server. Keeping the copyable commands in the README avoids typography changes in the blog breaking command-line flags.

Ask the agent to investigate Silicon Cascade, return the B/C dependency paths, plan and stress-test the recovery, execute in SIMULATION, and read the ERP state back. Then lower D to 450 units and compare the computed allocation. To examine the rejection branch separately, inject the one-shot simulated failure and explicitly inspect the failed and subsequent attempts.

Run uv run pytest for automated checks and uv run python scripts/benchmark.py to reproduce the benchmark on your own machine. Integration tests need a working local FalkorDB and can skip when it is unavailable; read the summary before claiming integration coverage.

What remains outside the prototype

The wider network supports traversal and visualization, not full optimization of all orders across a global business. The cascade calculation is a deterministic source/order allocation model. It does not propagate a complete bill of materials through multiple production facilities and time buckets. Some lead-time constraints exist, but that is not a complete scheduling or transport model.

All commercial facts are synthetic. Missing or incorrect upstream data limits the decision. Stress testing uses a finite set with simplified recourse and no event probabilities. Currency conversion, production authentication, remote service exposure, generalized compensation, and multi-process coordination are not implemented. Local HTTP/MCP is unauthenticated and intended for loopback use.

Production adoption would require validated operational data, broader constraints, controlled integrations, appropriate access and authorization, and independent review. Those are future requirements, not shipped capabilities.

The question Anchor leaves us with

The most useful question was not whether an agent could suggest a replacement supplier. It was whether we could follow its decision from a graph relationship to a feasible allocation, then through an attempted action to the persisted state.

In Silicon Cascade, the shared upstream path makes the cheap proposal infeasible. The optimizer chooses independent capacity. A capacity reduction changes the plan again. The executor records what happened, and read-back supplies a separate check. Each link is inspectable, including the cases where supply remains short or an action fails.

When an agent reports that it has completed a task, what evidence lets us verify that completion? Anchor is a small, synthetic experiment in making that question answerable.
